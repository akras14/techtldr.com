Margaret Hamilton, who led the MIT team that wrote the Apollo onboard flight software and helped make "software engineering" a recognized discipline, died on September 30 at 90. Her team's priority-driven design is why the Apollo 11 landing could continue through the famous 1202 alarm.

## Path into programming

- **Born** in Paoli, Indiana, in 1936. BA in math, with a philosophy minor, from Earlham College in 1958.
- **First programming job** in 1959, writing weather-prediction software for MIT meteorologist Edward Lorenz. That work fed into his later research on chaos theory.
- **SAGE air defense** at MIT Lincoln Lab from 1961. Writing software for the first U.S. air-defense system got her interested in software reliability, then a barely explored idea.
- **Apollo, 1965.** She answered a newspaper ad from MIT's Instrumentation Lab, which had the NASA contract for Apollo's onboard software. She became the project's first programmer, and its first woman programmer. By 1968 she led the command module software team, and more than 400 people were working on Apollo software.

## Two stories that defined her approach

**The "Lauren error."** Her four-year-old daughter, playing in the simulator, triggered a pre-launch program (P01) mid-flight and crashed the simulation. Hamilton proposed a software guard against this. She was overruled on the grounds that trained astronauts would never do it. On Apollo 8, Jim Lovell did exactly that and wiped the navigation data. Her fix went in afterwards. This is an early example of **defensive programming**: software that anticipates operator mistakes instead of trusting people not to make them.

**Apollo 11's 1202 alarm.** Moments before the lunar landing, the guidance computer signaled it was overloaded, caused by a faulty hardware switch setting. Because Hamilton's team had built **priority-driven scheduling**, the computer dropped low-priority work and kept the landing-critical tasks running. Mission control trusted the software, and the landing went ahead.

## After Apollo

- The Instrumentation Lab spun out of MIT as Draper Laboratory.
- In 1976 she founded **Higher Order Software**, built around error prevention and fault tolerance.
- A decade later she founded **Hamilton Technologies**. Its main product, the **Universal Systems Language**, is a modeling method for designing complex systems with errors prevented up front.
- She coined and championed the term **"software engineering"** to win software equal standing with hardware engineering. Early on, colleagues treated the phrase as a joke.

## Recognition

She received the Presidential Medal of Freedom (2016), NASA's Exceptional Space Act Award (2003), and the Computer History Museum Fellow Award (2017), and was inducted into the National Aviation Hall of Fame (2022). A photo of her beside a stack of printed Apollo code became an icon for women in tech. The Apollo source code went up on GitHub in 2015, and in 2017 she became a Lego minifigure in the Women of NASA set.

She authored more than 130 publications. A memorial service is planned for spring in Cambridge, Massachusetts.
