**Bottom line:** Iris Meredith argues that LLMs fail the test for a truly inevitable technology. They are closer to chemical weapons than to firearms: useful to weak players, marginal for serious ones, and easy to neutralise. Adoption will go on for contingent reasons, but no one is forced to use them.

**What "inevitable" means.** The author says a technology is inevitable only if four things hold:

1. It does the task about as well as the alternative.
2. The task is socially valuable and currently done by scarce labour.
3. It overwhelmingly outclasses the alternatives, not just moderately.
4. Non-adopters can't cheaply neutralise its impact.

Firearms pass all four: if you don't adopt them, you get absorbed by someone who does. The magnetic compass doesn't, because you can still navigate or simply not become a maritime power.

**How LLMs score.**

- **Quality:** They can't match top-tier work, but most human work is mediocre, so they roughly pass.
- **Social value:** The result is mixed. They replace the least valuable parts of skilled work, and the more important the work, the less useful they are.
- **Overwhelming advantage:** Clearly fails. Checking and directing the output limits the speedup, perhaps 20-50% on something like dashboards that aren't core bottlenecks. If the advantage were overwhelming, some tech company would already be shipping 5-10x as much as its rivals. Instead, the companies going all in on AI seem to be struggling more.
- **Neutralisation:** Humans can still match LLMs on useful output, and there is intense social opprobrium toward LLM use and "slop", which technologies rarely survive.

**The chemical weapons analogy.** Following Bret Devereaux, the author notes that top militaries almost never use chemical weapons. Their lethality doesn't help manoeuvre warfare, protection is cheap, and modern explosives do the job. Weaker powers fighting weaker powers can still find them useful. The author expects LLMs to follow the same pattern: second- and third-rate software shops will use them for a long time, serious practitioners will use them little or not at all, and LLM-backed cyberattacks and astroturfing are the "terror weapon" use.

**What it means.** If LLM effects can't be neutralised, adoption continues among weak players and the world gets bleaker, with everyone paying a cost to filter out LLM crud. If they can be reduced through regulation, economics and social pressure, skilled non-users should outcompete LLM users. Much of the current advantage rests on an irrational belief in LLM power, which the author expects to erode within a few years to a decade. The essay ends with a plug for the author's Arca project.
