**Bottom line:** The FDA is proposing to expand its "threshold of regulation" (TOR) exemption so that substances can be added directly to food, as flavorings, preservatives, emulsifiers, enzymes and more, without a food-additive safety review. Public health advocates say this lets potentially harmful chemicals onto grocery shelves, and that it was included quietly in the same proposed rule the FDA and Robert F. Kennedy Jr. publicized as closing the "generally recognized as safe" (GRAS) loophole.

## Details

- **Today's TOR:** Non-carcinogenic chemicals used in food contact materials (packaging, equipment) can skip review if they are added at under 0.5 parts per billion. Critics say many chemicals, including some hormone disruptors, phthalates, bisphenols, PFAS and flame retardants, can cause harm well below that level.
- **The proposed rule:** Any substance used in food, directly or indirectly, would be exempt if there is "no appreciable risk to human health", a term advocates say is poorly defined. It doesn't account for cumulative exposure to many small doses, and it creates a secondary loophole for carcinogens present as impurities. Consumers would have no way to know whether a product contains a TOR-exempted chemical.
- **Precedent:** A 2005 TOR exemption let BASF use the rocket-fuel chemical perchlorate in grain "super sacks". FDA data later showed perchlorate in kids' cereal increased, and perchlorate is linked to reduced thyroid function and lower IQ in children. The FDA reapproved the use anyway.
- **Politics:** Kennedy campaigned on fixing GRAS and the FDA declared "promises kept" in August. Critics call the GRAS change a modest tightening that relies on voluntary compliance, and say the TOR expansion offsets it. It is unclear who added the TOR expansion. The FDA didn't respond to a request for comment.
