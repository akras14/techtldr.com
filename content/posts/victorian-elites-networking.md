Victorian Britain's ruling class barely studied science, slept late, drank heavily, and spent half the year at balls, yet they ran what was for much of the century the world's richest country, along with a vast empire. Samuel Hughes argues their real work was building one tight-knit community through shared schooling and nonstop socializing. That community kept the elite united, and let it pay people in prestige instead of money.

Hughes is careful not to overclaim. You can't run a controlled experiment on Victorian Britain, and the elite may simply have lived off the Industrial Revolution's wealth. His point is narrower: today's success playbook of hard STEM, 5am starts, and long hours isn't the only one that has ever worked.

## School: soft skills, sports, and self-government

- **Boarding from about age 8.** Elite boys grew up with each other more than with family, which was unusual even in Europe at the time. Hughes suggests this is part of why Britain's elite never split along regional lines.
- **A curriculum that barely mattered.** Latin and Greek filled half or more of class time, science was resisted, and most graduates forgot their classics anyway. Leading educators openly ranked character above intellect.
- **Fewer classroom hours** than schools today or than elite schools in France and Germany.
- **10–20 hours a week of team sports**, valued for loyalty, endurance, and learning to win and lose well. This is where today's American school sports culture comes from.
- **Prefects:** older boys ran discipline and pastoral care. It was sometimes brutal, but it was designed as an apprenticeship in leadership, with authority coming from peers rather than masters.

## University: Oxbridge as a social finishing school

- **Almost everyone who applied got in.** Many students, especially aristocrats, took an easy "pass" degree and spent their time rowing and dining.
- **Even serious students** got a general moral and intellectual education rather than job training. As late as 1910–14, only about 14% of honors degrees were in science.
- **Students lived together in colleges**, unlike students in continental Europe. Days were chapel, reading, sports, tutorials over sherry, and formal dinner.
- **A colonial training college** for the Indian civil service was widely mocked as a place to learn. Even so, its graduates agreed it created strong loyalty, which a future prime minister said turned later rivalries into real cooperation.

## Adulthood: late mornings, long nights

- **Late starts.** Gladstone's breakfasts began at 10:30 with claret, and Balfour rarely left his bedroom before lunch.
- **A few hours at the office**, a long club lunch with wine, and Parliament convening around 4:30pm, since most MPs were part-timers.
- **Elaborate multi-course dinners** with a different wine per course, and heavy drinking by both men and women.
- **The Season** (February to July): the elite lived within walking distance of each other in Mayfair and Belgravia and attended balls, soirées, and supper parties almost every night, often until dawn.

Hughes argues this wasn't idleness. Balls mixed debutantes with cabinet ministers, bankers, and judges, acting as a teenage party and a business networking event at once. Personal and professional life were almost completely merged, and burnout was common.

## Why it might have worked

Hughes uses Tönnies' distinction between *Gemeinschaft* (a community held together by sentiment) and *Gesellschaft* (a society held together by contracts). The Victorian elite was a village-sized community at the top of a huge contract-based society. He sees two benefits:

1. **Unity.** Landowners and industrialists, Anglicans, Nonconformists, and Jews were absorbed into one elite instead of forming rival elites. Ireland was the major exception. Compare the American South's split from the North, or the class and regional conflicts in Spain, Italy, France, and Germany.
2. **Paying people in prestige.** Local government, magistrates, and school and hospital boards were run by unpaid landowners. MPs were unpaid and covered their own election costs, around £150k–£450k in today's money per election, and seats were still fiercely contested. The judgment of their peers was enough to keep people serving, and Hughes suggests it may also have restrained partisan attacks that hurt the country.

He ends with a thought experiment: what if the richest tech founders competed for seats on San Francisco's Board of Supervisors because it was the most prestigious thing they could do? It might be a disaster, he says, but it would certainly be interesting.
