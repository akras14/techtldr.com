Carson Gross, who created htmx and teaches computer science at Montana State, tells students that programming is still a good career, with a condition: write the code yourself, because you can't review or architect what you never learned to write. He suggests using AI as a teaching assistant rather than a code generator, and building communication, business and architecture skills alongside.

## "Yes"

Programming is problem-solving with computers and controlling the complexity of the solutions. He can't picture those skills becoming less valuable.

## "You have to write the code"

- He tells students: AI can do this assignment; don't let it. If you don't write code, you can't read it, and reading code may matter more with AI.
- He rejects the "prompting is like moving from assembly to high-level languages" comparison. Compilers are mostly deterministic and remove accidental complexity; LLM output isn't deterministic and often adds accidental complexity. You can't spot that if you can't read code.
- **AI as a TA:** it's very good at getting students unstuck, especially on accidental complexity like toolchains. He shares an AGENTS.md that configures agents to tutor rather than write the code.

## "…and"

Raw coding skill may lose relative value, so he suggests building:

- **Communication:** writing and thinking clearly, for humans and LLMs.
- **Business understanding:** AI may free programmers to learn the domain they're solving for.
- **Architecture:** organizing large systems and controlling their complexity. Good architects come from coding experience, which is another reason to write code.
- **Using LLMs well.** His own uses: analyzing code, organizing thoughts, small code pieces, code he dislikes writing (regex, CSS), throwaway demos, and suggesting tests. He doesn't have LLMs write full solutions he'll maintain, and never lets them design his APIs.

Seniors risk "brain rot" and doomscrolling while waiting on prompts. Juniors face peers who vibe-code faster; he expects companies to rediscover that deliberate coding beats complexity blowups. His message to companies: let juniors write at least some of the code.

## Getting a job now

He calls the job market bad but cyclical. His advice: online job boards are mostly a lottery; use "Family, Friends & Family of Friends." Almost any company over 100 people has a development group, and starting in another role (say, analyst at a parent's employer) plus programming skill can launch a career.
