**Bottom line:** Driverless trucks are now operating commercially, and regulation is loosening, but the economics and safety claims are less tidy than the startups say. The author thinks even 5 percent adoption of Level 4 trucks by 2035 is aspirational, and that real trucking safety depends on regulation and public investment more than on better drivers.

## State of play

- Regulatory barriers are falling: California allowed autonomous truck testing in April, the DOT approved cab-mounted warning beacons in place of triangles, and a federal framework is in the Build America 250 Act.
- Aurora leads, with about 200 trucks promised by year-end, 500,000 driverless miles, and a plan to upfit about 1,000 trucks a year from 2027. Kodiak has 35 customer-owned trucks, mostly hauling sand in the Permian Basin. Others include Waabi, Bot Auto, Gatik and Daimler's Torc.
- The industry wants Level 4 (no human in the cab). Level 3 does not pencil out because the savings come from removing the driver.

## Economics

- Pitch: Aurora's "Driver-as-a-Service" target is $0.85 per mile, against $1.03 per mile for driver pay (about 17 percent less). So far it only runs a Transportation-as-a-Service model, at roughly $2 plus per mile.
- Forecasts vary widely. McKinsey says 13 percent of trucks by 2035, Goldman roughly 5.5 percent, and Aurora about 170,000 trucks.
- Open questions: who pays for yard work, trailer handling, refueling, breakdowns and insurance, and how liability plays out in lawsuits, since no one has been sued yet. Analysts note that the forecasts come from companies that need to build consensus.

## Safety

The author says the claim that machines beat flawed humans ignores why aviation is safe: heavy regulation and funding (the FAA has $25 billion and over 4,000 inspectors; the FMCSA has under $1 billion and 350 investigators). Trucking kills over 5,000 people a year, 94 percent of interstate carriers have no FMCSA safety rating, and about one in five inspected trucks is placed out of service. Without changed incentives for maintenance, he argues, autonomous systems will not fix those problems on their own.
