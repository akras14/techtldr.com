**Bottom line:** The Pentagon has announced plans to livestream the firing squad execution of Army officer Nidal Hasan, without saying on which platform. Masnick sets aside the debate over capital punishment (he calls it barbaric) and asks a narrower question: would hosting the stream break the platforms' own rules, and would they enforce them? Streaming it is probably legal in the US, but the policies are another matter.

## Platform by platform

- **X:** Has a violent content policy, including a ban on violent content in live video and on glorification, but with wiggle room ("may" remove, "excessively gory"). Masnick expects the stream to be shared and any decision to be subjective. A "moment of death" clause lets a family request removal, which X is not obliged to honor.
- **YouTube:** The stream would appear to violate its ban on violent content meant to shock. But YouTube allows public-interest exceptions, and lists "government proceedings" as an example, so it has an out.
- **Meta (Facebook/Instagram):** Its policy explicitly forbids "live-streams of capital punishments," with no obvious exception. Hosting the stream would mean ignoring or changing the policy, which Masnick notes comes after Meta spent years courting the current administration.
- **TikTok:** Has a less detailed policy; the post continues on this and other platforms.

## Takeaway

The test is whether policies written for general use survive pressure from a government that wants its own violence broadcast. Masnick's view is that the outcome will show whether such rules are principles or just convenient language.
