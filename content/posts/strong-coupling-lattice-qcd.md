Of the Standard Model's three gauge couplings, the strong coupling alpha_s is the least well known, at about one part in a hundred, versus better than one in a billion for electromagnetism. As LHC measurements reach percent-level precision, this has become a theoretical bottleneck, and the High-Luminosity LHC and a future Higgs factory such as FCC-ee will need more. The new result needs no collider data as input, so it can feed collider predictions without circularity.

The difficulty is that the coupling grows strong at low energies, where quarks and gluons are confined in hadrons and perturbation theory fails. Lattice QCD, introduced by Kenneth Wilson in 1974, puts spacetime on a grid and samples field configurations statistically, and it has become mature enough to settle questions such as the muon g-2 prediction.

Measuring alpha_s directly on a lattice would need one large enough to hold hadrons yet fine enough to resolve energies above roughly 70 GeV, which no computer can do. The step-scaling method of Lüscher, Weisz and Wolff avoids this with a family of small simulated "femto-universes", each half the size and twice the energy of the last, and follows the coupling upward until perturbation theory takes over.

The ALPHA paper (Nature, April 2026) inputs only the pion and kaon masses and a benchmark length of about 0.14 fm. It simulates the three lightest quarks and adds the effect of charm and bottom afterwards with perturbative results. Its value agrees with the Particle Data Group average.
