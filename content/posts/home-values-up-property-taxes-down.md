Taylor points to David Schleicher's paper "The Great American Property Tax Freak Out" (SSRN, September 2026). Over the last three years several states have reformed property taxes to give big breaks to owner-occupied homes, moving the load onto commercial property (including rental apartment buildings), other local taxes and state funding. Florida, Ohio, North Dakota and Texas have considered going further, up to abolishing the tax on owner-occupied homes or on property altogether.

Schleicher's reading: property tax is a wealth tax, so homeowners whose homes rose in value after COVID responded with political anger. The reforms turn the tax from a way for homeowners to collectively fund local services into ordinary redistribution, with commercial owners paying for homeowners' services. Expected effects are more state control over local governments, less stable local funding (but fewer tax foreclosures in recessions), stricter zoning (but more building where it is allowed), and higher housing costs. Net, it is a transfer of wealth to people who already own homes.

Taylor adds three points:

- Property taxes are the main own-source revenue for local governments and schools, and often police. Voters demanding cuts may not have connected them to reduced services.
- As a wealth tax, it hits people whose wealth grows without matching income, such as older homeowners, who are a powerful group in this fight.
- Lower property taxes lower the cost of owning, which raises home prices. So rising prices fuel the push to cut taxes, and the cuts push prices higher still, benefiting current owners and making ownership costlier for future buyers.
