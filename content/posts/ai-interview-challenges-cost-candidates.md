A startup head of engineering points out a new cost of job hunting. Many companies now expect candidates to use LLMs in technical challenges, but good coding models need a paid subscription or API credits, and candidates pay for them while they have no income. Company accounts and reimbursement don't work, so the author changes the process: hold the challenge until the company is seriously interested, scope it to run on a free or basic tier, do it live on the company's own AI account, and weigh an architecture conversation more than the code.

The author's team expects candidates to use LLMs, because they see it as a core part of software engineering now. Seen from the candidate's side, job hunting is a numbers game. Someone might interview with five companies in one week, and each one hands out a challenge that assumes AI help. The usual reply is that getting a job has always cost money. The author's answer is that housing, food, and clothes have uses beyond getting hired, while tokens spent on a take-home are used up and serve no other purpose.

The obvious fixes don't hold up. Lending candidates a company account is a liability, since the company can't control how it gets used. Paying back what candidates spent is hard to justify as a business expense and becomes a financial gray area.

So the author changes the parts of the process the company controls:

- **Move the challenge later.** Candidates spend money only after a real conversation, when an offer is more likely.
- **Make it smaller.** A challenge that needs a top-tier model running for hours tests the candidate's wallet. A tight problem that works on a free or basic plan, followed by a talk about the approach, shows more.
- **Do it together.** This is the author's favorite. The candidate drives a live session while the AI runs on the company's machine and account, so there is no liability and nothing to pay back. It also shows how the candidate reacts when the model is confidently wrong.
- **Weigh the code less.** When an LLM writes most of the code, the code says less about the person. A conversation about system structure, boundaries, testing, and shipping says more and costs the candidate nothing.

The author admits each option has a downside: live sessions stress some people, and a smaller challenge gives less signal. They still prefer to pick a trade-off on purpose, and they ask other hiring managers to work out what their process costs someone with no income.
